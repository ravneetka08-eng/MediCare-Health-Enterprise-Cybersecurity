# 1. Purpose

This methodology establishes the process used by MediCare Health
Australia to identify, analyse, evaluate, treat, accept, monitor,
review and report cybersecurity risks.

The methodology is intended to ensure that cybersecurity risk
decisions:

- are based on documented business context;
- consider clinical operations and patient safety;
- consider information confidentiality, integrity and availability;
- consider privacy and regulatory consequences;
- identify accountable risk owners;
- evaluate existing security controls;
- distinguish inherent risk from residual risk;
- define measurable treatment actions;
- require evidence where control effectiveness is claimed;
- identify security exceptions and compensating controls;
- establish appropriate escalation and acceptance requirements; and
- maintain traceability from risk identification through closure.

Cybersecurity risk management is not treated as a one-time compliance
exercise.

Risks are expected to change as:

- technology changes;
- vulnerabilities are discovered;
- threat activity changes;
- business services change;
- clinical systems change;
- cloud services are introduced;
- third parties change;
- controls are implemented or fail;
- incidents occur.

---

# 2. Objectives

The cybersecurity risk-management process aims to:

1. identify cyber risks before they result in unacceptable harm;
2. prioritise security investment according to business risk;
3. protect critical healthcare services and information;
4. support informed decision-making by accountable risk owners;
5. identify security-control weaknesses;
6. track remediation through to completion;
7. evaluate whether controls operate as intended;
8. document residual risk after treatment;
9. ensure accepted risks are explicitly owned and reviewed;
10. provide management with meaningful risk reporting;
11. support incident, vulnerability and security-exception processes;
12. maintain evidence supporting material risk decisions.

# 4. Risk Governance Model

Cybersecurity risks are not owned solely by the cybersecurity team.

The business area that receives the benefit from an activity or
service generally retains accountability for risks associated with
that activity.

Cybersecurity provides specialist risk advice, assessment,
monitoring and control assurance.

## 4.1 Board / Executive Management

Responsibilities include:

- oversight of significant enterprise cyber risk;
- consideration of cyber risk in organisational strategy;
- review of material risk exposure;
- oversight of significant cyber incidents;
- ensuring appropriate risk-management capability exists.

---

## 4.2 Chief Information Security Officer

Responsibilities include:

- maintaining the cybersecurity risk-management framework;
- establishing security standards;
- overseeing significant cyber-risk assessments;
- escalating material risks;
- advising executives and risk owners;
- monitoring aggregate cyber-risk exposure;
- overseeing cybersecurity treatment programs.

---

## 4.3 Cybersecurity / GRC Function

Responsibilities include:

- facilitating risk assessments;
- maintaining the cyber risk register;
- supporting risk scenario development;
- assessing security controls;
- challenging unsupported risk assumptions;
- tracking treatment actions;
- monitoring overdue actions;
- coordinating security exceptions;
- preparing risk reporting;
- retaining risk evidence;
- facilitating periodic risk review.

---

## 4.4 Cybersecurity Analyst

Responsibilities may include:

- identifying potential cybersecurity risks;
- gathering technical evidence;
- identifying vulnerabilities and threats;
- assessing technical control gaps;
- supporting likelihood and impact assessment;
- recommending treatment options;
- testing implemented controls;
- validating remediation;
- documenting findings.

The analyst does not automatically have authority to accept material
business risk.

---

## 4.5 Risk Owner

The Risk Owner is accountable for managing a particular risk.

The Risk Owner should have sufficient organisational authority to:

- understand the affected business service;
- approve treatment priorities;
- allocate or request resources;
- understand operational consequences;
- accept residual risk where authorised;
- escalate risks outside their authority.

Risk ownership must be assigned to a role capable of making decisions
about the affected business service.

A cybersecurity analyst should not be assigned as the owner of every
business risk merely because the risk is cyber-related.

---

## 4.6 Control Owner

The Control Owner is accountable for ensuring a particular control is:

- appropriately designed;
- implemented;
- operated;
- maintained;
- monitored;
- evidenced.

Example:

Risk:
Compromise of workforce credentials.

Risk Owner:
Business or technology service owner.

Control:
Active Directory account lifecycle management.

Control Owner:
Identity / IT Infrastructure function.

---

## 4.7 Treatment Action Owner

The Treatment Action Owner is responsible for completing a specific
risk-treatment action.

Example:

Risk:
Unpatched critical server vulnerability.

Risk Owner:
Clinical Systems Owner.

Treatment Action:
Deploy approved security update.

Action Owner:
Windows Infrastructure Team.

Cybersecurity may then independently rescan the system to verify the
treatment.

# 5. Three Lines Concept

The project recognises the principle of separating:

## First Line

Functions that own and operate systems and business processes.

Examples:

- IT Infrastructure;
- Clinical Systems;
- Cloud Operations;
- application owners.

They operate many of the controls.

## Second Line

Functions that provide specialist risk oversight.

Examples:

- Cybersecurity Governance;
- Risk;
- Compliance;
- Privacy.

They establish requirements, provide challenge and monitor risk.

## Third Line

Independent assurance.

Example:

Internal Audit.

The student project cannot reproduce true organisational independence.

Control testing performed by the student is therefore classified as
self-assessed control assurance rather than independent audit.

Cybersecurity risks may be categorised using one or more of the

| Category | Description |
|---|---|
| Identity and Access | Authentication, authorisation, privilege and account lifecycle |

| Server Security | Server configuration, patching and protection |

| Data Protection | Confidentiality, integrity and appropriate information handling |
| Vulnerability Management | Identification and remediation of vulnerabilities |
| Security Monitoring | Logging, detection and alerting |
| Incident Response | Investigation, containment and recovery capability |
| Backup and Recovery | Backup protection and restoration capability |
| Third-Party Risk | Suppliers, vendors and external service providers |

| Security Governance | Policy, ownership, assurance and risk-management weaknesses |
| Human Risk | Social engineering, errors and inappropriate user behaviour |
| Privacy | Unauthorised access, use or disclosure of personal information |

A risk may belong to multiple categories.

# 8. Risk Scenario Structure

Risks should be written as scenarios rather than vague technical
statements.

Preferred structure:

There is a risk that [THREAT / EVENT]

exploits or occurs because of [VULNERABILITY / CONTROL WEAKNESS]

affecting [ASSET / BUSINESS SERVICE]

resulting in [BUSINESS / CLINICAL / PRIVACY CONSEQUENCE].

Example:

There is a risk that an external threat actor obtains valid workforce
credentials because of inadequate authentication protection and uses
the compromised identity to access restricted clinical information,
resulting in unauthorised disclosure, operational disruption and
privacy consequences.

Poor risk statement:

"Phishing risk."

The detailed scenario is preferred because it explains:

- what might happen;
- why it might happen;
- what would be affected;
- why the organisation should care.

# 10. Inherent Risk

Inherent Risk represents the level of risk assuming relevant
risk-reducing controls are absent or ineffective.

It answers:

"What would the exposure look like if we did not rely on our current
controls?"

Inherent risk helps management understand the underlying importance
of the risk.

Inherent Risk is assessed using:

Inherent Likelihood × Inherent Impact

---

# 11. Likelihood Assessment

Likelihood is assessed using available evidence and professional
judgement.

Factors may include:

- threat capability;
- threat intent;
- frequency of similar events;
- system exposure;
- attack surface;
- exploitability;
- existing vulnerabilities;
- accessibility;
- complexity of attack;
- historical incidents;
- industry threat activity;
- attractiveness of the target.

## Likelihood Scale

### 1 — Rare

The event would require exceptional circumstances or is considered
highly unusual in the defined context.

### 2 — Unlikely

The event is plausible but is not expected to occur under normal
circumstances.

### 3 — Possible

The event could occur and credible circumstances exist that could
produce the scenario.

### 4 — Likely

The event has a realistic and significant opportunity to occur.

### 5 — Almost Certain

The event is expected to occur repeatedly or circumstances make the
scenario highly probable.

Likelihood ratings must include documented rationale for material
risks.

---

# 12. Impact Assessment

Impact must not be determined only by technical severity.

Healthcare cyber risk can create consequences across several
dimensions.

The following impact dimensions are considered:

1. Clinical / Patient Care
2. Information Confidentiality
3. Information Integrity
4. Service Availability
5. Privacy
6. Operational
7. Financial
8. Legal / Regulatory
9. Reputation

---

# 13. Clinical / Patient-Care Impact

## 1 — Insignificant

No meaningful impact to clinical service delivery.

## 2 — Minor

Limited inconvenience or short disruption with established
workarounds available.

## 3 — Moderate

Noticeable disruption to a clinical workflow requiring operational
intervention.

## 4 — Major

Significant disruption to important clinical services or information
availability requiring major operational response.

## 5 — Severe

Potential for widespread or sustained disruption to critical
healthcare services or circumstances requiring urgent executive and
clinical management attention.

Cybersecurity personnel must not independently make clinical
patient-safety determinations outside their expertise.

Relevant clinical stakeholders should be consulted where a risk may
affect patient care.

---

# 14. Confidentiality Impact

## 1 — Insignificant

No sensitive information exposed.

## 2 — Minor

Limited internal information exposure.

## 3 — Moderate

Unauthorised disclosure of confidential organisational information.

## 4 — Major

Significant exposure of sensitive information.

## 5 — Severe

Large-scale or highly sensitive restricted information exposure,
including significant patient-information exposure.

---

# 15. Integrity Impact

## 1 — Insignificant

No meaningful information modification.

## 2 — Minor

Limited incorrect information with easy correction.

## 3 — Moderate

Material information integrity issue requiring investigation.

## 4 — Major

Significant modification of important operational or clinical data.

## 5 — Severe

Loss of trust in critical information or widespread unauthorised
modification of critical data.

---

# 16. Availability Impact

## 1 — Insignificant

Negligible interruption.

## 2 — Minor

Short interruption with minimal business impact.

## 3 — Moderate

Material service degradation or outage affecting business processes.

## 4 — Major

Significant outage affecting important organisational or clinical
services.

## 5 — Severe

Sustained or widespread loss of critical services requiring major
business continuity or incident response.

---

# 17. Privacy Impact

Privacy impact considers:

- sensitivity of information;
- volume of information;
- number of individuals potentially affected;
- nature of unauthorised access or disclosure;
- potential harm;
- ability to contain the event;
- applicable privacy obligations.

Privacy-impact assessment should involve the Privacy function where
appropriate.

---

# 18. Overall Impact Determination

The overall impact rating should reflect the most significant credible
consequence while considering the complete business context.

A risk must not be artificially averaged downward merely because some
impact dimensions are low.

Example:

Financial Impact = Moderate

Clinical Availability Impact = Severe

The Severe clinical consequence should not disappear because the
financial consequence is lower.

The rationale for the selected impact rating must be documented for
material risks.

# 19. Risk Matrix

Risk Score:

Likelihood × Impact

| Likelihood \ Impact | 1 Insignificant | 2 Minor | 3 Moderate | 4 Major | 5 Severe |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 5 Almost Certain | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 |
| 4 Likely | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 | 20 |
| 3 Possible | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 | 15 |
| 2 Unlikely | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
| 1 Rare | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |

## Rating Bands

| Score | Rating |
|---|---|
| 1–4 | Low |
| 5–9 | Medium |
| 10–16 | High |
| 17–25 | Critical |

The numerical score supports prioritisation.

It does not replace professional judgement.

A risk may require escalation despite its calculated score where:

- patient care may be materially affected;
- significant privacy consequences are possible;
- a critical regulatory obligation is affected;
- a crown-jewel asset is exposed;
- multiple control failures exist;
- active exploitation is occurring;
- executive management determines escalation is required.

# 20. Existing Control Identification

Before residual risk is assessed, relevant existing controls must be
identified.

Controls may be:

## Preventive

Designed to stop an event.

Examples:

- MFA;
- least privilege;
- network segmentation;
- secure configuration.

## Detective

Designed to identify an event.

Examples:

- SIEM detection;
- authentication monitoring;
- file-access auditing.

## Corrective

Designed to remediate an identified weakness.

Examples:

- patch deployment;
- account disablement;
- configuration correction.

## Recovery

Designed to restore capability.

Examples:

- backup restoration;
- disaster recovery;
- account recovery.

## Compensating

Alternative controls used where the preferred control cannot be
implemented.

Example:

An unsupported clinical device cannot be patched.

Compensating controls may include:

- isolated network segment;
- strict firewall rules;
- restricted administration;
- enhanced monitoring;
- vendor-supported configuration;
- replacement roadmap.

---

# 21. Control Design Assessment

Control Design Effectiveness considers whether the control, if
operated as intended, is capable of reducing the identified risk.

Possible assessments:

- Effective Design
- Partially Effective Design
- Ineffective Design
- Not Assessed
- Not Applicable

Example:

A password policy alone may be insufficiently designed to address a
risk scenario involving stolen credentials if stronger authentication
is required.

---

# 22. Control Operating Effectiveness

Operating Effectiveness considers whether the control actually
operates as intended.

Possible assessments:

- Effective
- Partially Effective
- Ineffective
- Not Tested
- Insufficient Evidence

A control must not be marked Effective solely because:

- a policy exists;
- a setting appears enabled;
- a product is installed.

Where practical, operating effectiveness should be supported by
evidence.

Example:

Control:
Former employee accounts are disabled promptly.

Weak evidence:
"HR policy says accounts are disabled."

Stronger project evidence:

1. test former.employee account exists;
2. termination workflow is executed;
3. account is disabled;
4. authentication is attempted;
5. authentication fails;
6. relevant event is logged;
7. evidence is retained.

---

# 23. Control Evidence

Control-effectiveness claims should reference evidence where practical.

Examples:

- configuration export;
- command output;
- event log;
- SIEM event;
- vulnerability scan;
- access-control test;
- screenshot;
- policy;
- approval record;
- recovery-test result.

Project evidence should reference:

EV-XXX

Example:

Existing Control:
Privileged accounts are separated from standard accounts.

Evidence:

EV-041 — Privileged account configuration

EV-042 — Standard user privilege test

EV-043 — Administrative authentication event

---

# 24. Residual Risk

Residual Risk represents the risk remaining after existing or
implemented controls are considered.

Residual risk must not automatically be assumed to be Low merely
because controls exist.

Assessment should consider:

- control design;
- control operating effectiveness;
- control coverage;
- evidence quality;
- known exceptions;
- unresolved vulnerabilities;
- remaining threat exposure.

Residual Risk is recorded using:

Residual Likelihood × Residual Impact

The rationale must explain why the rating changed or did not change
from inherent risk.

---

# 25. Risk Appetite

Risk Appetite represents the type and amount of risk the organisation
is prepared to pursue or retain while delivering its objectives.

For this fictional healthcare scenario, the following principles are
used:

The organisation has very low appetite for risks that could result in:

- material patient-safety consequences;
- significant unauthorised patient-information disclosure;
- compromise of privileged enterprise identities;
- uncontrolled access to critical clinical systems;
- inability to recover critical services;
- deliberate non-compliance with material security obligations.

Some operational cybersecurity risk may be tolerated where:

- immediate remediation could create greater clinical risk;
- a legacy clinical dependency prevents immediate remediation;
- compensating controls materially reduce exposure;
- a documented treatment plan exists;
- an authorised Risk Owner accepts the residual exposure;
- the exception is time-limited and reviewed.

Risk appetite does not mean that all cyber risk can be eliminated.

---

# 26. Risk Treatment Options

## Reduce / Mitigate

Implement controls to reduce likelihood and/or impact.

Example:

Implement MFA to reduce account-compromise risk.

## Avoid

Stop the activity creating the unacceptable risk.

Example:

Decommission an unnecessary exposed service.

## Transfer / Share

Transfer or share elements of financial or operational exposure
through contractual arrangements, insurance or service arrangements.

Transfer does not automatically remove accountability for
cybersecurity risk.

## Accept

Formally retain the residual risk.

Acceptance requires:

- documented rationale;
- authorised Risk Owner;
- residual risk rating;
- supporting evidence;
- review date;
- expiry where appropriate.

Acceptance must not be used simply because remediation is difficult.

---

# 27. Risk Treatment Plan

Where treatment is required, the plan should record:

- Risk ID;
- treatment objective;
- individual treatment actions;
- action owner;
- control owner;
- priority;
- target date;
- dependencies;
- resource requirements where relevant;
- expected risk reduction;
- validation method;
- evidence requirement;
- implementation status;
- blockers;
- revised target date where applicable.

Treatment actions should be specific and testable.

Poor treatment:

"Improve security."

Better treatment:

"Remove Reception users from the local Administrators group on
managed Reception workstations and verify membership through
centralised configuration and control testing."

---

# 28. Compensating Controls

Where the preferred control cannot be implemented, the Risk Owner and
Cybersecurity function should consider whether alternative controls
can sufficiently reduce the risk.

A compensating control record should explain:

1. required control;
2. reason the control cannot be implemented;
3. affected systems;
4. resulting exposure;
5. compensating control;
6. control owner;
7. evidence;
8. residual risk;
9. expiry/review date;
10. long-term remediation plan.

Compensating controls should not silently become permanent.

---

# 29. Security Exceptions

A security exception is required where an approved deviation from a
mandatory security requirement exists.

Exceptions should link to:

- affected Risk ID;
- affected Control ID;
- affected Asset ID;
- business justification;
- compensating controls;
- Risk Owner;
- approval;
- residual risk;
- expiry date;
- remediation plan.

Exceptions will be maintained within the project's
Security Exception Register.

---

# 30. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance represents a conscious decision to retain residual
risk.

Acceptance should occur only after:

- the risk is understood;
- treatment alternatives are considered;
- residual exposure is documented;
- relevant control limitations are understood;
- an authorised person accepts accountability;
- a review date is established.

Cybersecurity provides advice but should not silently accept business
risk on behalf of accountable business owners.

---

|---|---|

# 32. Risk Status

Risks may use the following statuses:

## Draft

Risk is being assessed.

## Open

Risk has been validated and requires management.

## Treatment Planned

Treatment has been approved but implementation has not begun.

## In Treatment

Treatment actions are being implemented.

## Pending Validation

Treatment is reportedly complete and requires control validation.

## Monitoring

Residual risk is being actively monitored.

## Accepted

Residual risk has been formally accepted by authorised ownership.

## Closed

Risk has been reduced, removed or otherwise resolved according to
defined closure criteria.

## Reopened

New evidence or changed circumstances require reassessment.

---

# 33. Risk Review Triggers

A risk should be reviewed when:

- its scheduled review date is reached;
- a significant vulnerability is discovered;
- a security incident occurs;
- control testing fails;
- a major architecture change occurs;
- a new cloud service is introduced;
- a critical supplier changes;
- threat conditions materially change;
- a treatment deadline is missed;
- an exception expires;
- a system is decommissioned;
- new regulatory or contractual requirements apply;
- evidence indicates the existing rating is no longer appropriate.

---

# 34. Risk Review Frequency

Representative minimum review expectations:

Critical:
At least monthly while unresolved.

High:
At least quarterly or more frequently while significant treatment
remains outstanding.

Medium:
At least six-monthly.

Low:
At least annually.

Review frequency may be increased based on:

- active exploitation;
- incident activity;
- clinical importance;
- regulatory significance;
- treatment delays;
- control failures.

---

# 35. Risk Escalation

Risks should be escalated where:

- residual risk exceeds appetite;
- treatment is materially overdue;
- the Risk Owner lacks authority to accept the exposure;
- a control failure materially increases exposure;
- patient-care impact may be significant;
- widespread restricted information exposure is possible;
- active exploitation is detected;
- multiple related risks create aggregate exposure;
- management intervention is required.

---

# 36. Risk Aggregation

Individual risks should not always be considered independently.

Several moderate or high risks may combine into a more significant
enterprise exposure.

Example:

R-001 Weak authentication

+

R-002 Excessive privilege

+

R-008 Insufficient monitoring

may collectively create a significantly greater identity-compromise
scenario than any single control weakness viewed alone.

Risk reporting should therefore consider common:

- assets;
- threat actors;
- control weaknesses;
- business services;
- technology dependencies.

---

# 37. Key Risk Indicators

Representative KRIs may include:

- number of Critical risks;
- number of High risks;
- risks exceeding appetite;
- overdue risk-treatment actions;
- expired security exceptions;
- critical vulnerabilities beyond remediation SLA;
- privileged accounts without required controls;
- inactive accounts remaining enabled;
- critical systems not reporting security telemetry;
- backup recovery-test failures;
- cloud resources with high-risk misconfiguration.

Thresholds will be developed as project controls mature.

---

# 38. Key Performance Indicators

Representative cybersecurity risk-management KPIs may include:

- percentage of treatment actions completed by target date;
- percentage of Critical/High risks reviewed on schedule;
- percentage of control tests completed;
- percentage of remediation actions successfully validated;
- average age of unresolved High/Critical risks;
- percentage of security exceptions reviewed before expiry.

A KPI measures performance.

A KRI indicates risk exposure.

The two should not be treated as interchangeable.

---

# 39. Risk Closure Criteria

A risk should not be closed merely because a remediation ticket was
marked complete.

Closure should require evidence that:

1. approved treatment was implemented;
2. relevant controls were validated where practical;
3. evidence was retained;
4. residual risk was reassessed;
5. the Risk Owner agrees closure criteria are satisfied;
6. outstanding exceptions are addressed;
7. required documentation is updated.

A risk may remain open at a lower residual rating if exposure still
requires ongoing management.

---

# 40. Risk Reopening

A previously closed or accepted risk may be reopened where:

- the control fails;
- new vulnerabilities appear;
- threat conditions change;
- an incident demonstrates greater exposure;
- architecture changes invalidate previous assumptions;
- treatment is reversed;
- supporting evidence is no longer valid.

---

# 41. Risk Evidence and Audit Trail

Material risk decisions should maintain traceability.

Representative records include:

- original risk assessment;
- rating rationale;
- evidence reviewed;
- treatment decisions;
- approvals;
- exceptions;
- changes in ownership;
- target-date changes;
- control-test results;
- residual-risk assessment;
- acceptance decisions;
- closure evidence.

Risk history should not be silently overwritten where maintaining the
decision trail is important.

---

# 42. Relationship to Vulnerability Management

A vulnerability is not automatically equivalent to a business risk.

Example:

Vulnerability:

Critical CVSS vulnerability on a server.

Risk assessment additionally considers:

- what server is affected;
- whether exploitation is possible;
- network exposure;
- available controls;
- business function;
- clinical dependency;
- data sensitivity;
- exploit evidence;
- remediation feasibility.

Vulnerability management provides important input into risk
management, but risk decisions require business context.

---

# 43. Relationship to Incident Management

An incident may:

- create a new risk;
- demonstrate that an existing risk was underestimated;
- demonstrate control failure;
- trigger urgent treatment;
- change residual-risk ratings.

Lessons learned from incidents should therefore feed back into the
risk register.

---

# 44. Relationship to Change Management

Risk treatments affecting production-like services should consider
change-management requirements.

Examples:

- patching;
- firewall changes;
- authentication changes;
- Group Policy;
- service configuration;
- network segmentation.

In a real healthcare environment, technically correct remediation may
still require:

- change approval;
- maintenance window;
- rollback plan;
- application testing;
- clinical coordination;
- post-change validation.

The student laboratory will simulate relevant elements of this
process where useful.

---

# 45. Relationship to Privacy

Cybersecurity risks involving personal or health information should
be evaluated for privacy implications.

Cybersecurity and Privacy responsibilities overlap but are not
identical.

Cybersecurity may determine:

- how access occurred;
- which systems were affected;
- what security controls failed.

Privacy specialists may determine:

- privacy impact;
- notification considerations;
- obligations affecting personal information.

The project will document these functions separately where relevant.

---

# 46. Relationship to Business Continuity

Cyber risks affecting availability must consider:

- critical business services;
- recovery priorities;
- backup availability;
- recovery capability;
- operational workarounds;
- service dependencies.

Cybersecurity risk treatment should therefore integrate with backup,
recovery and continuity planning.

# 47. Project Evidence Integration

Risk records within this project may link to:

AST-XXX — Asset

CJ-XXX — Crown Jewel

TM-XXX — Threat Scenario

R-XXX — Risk

CTRL-XXX — Security Control

TEST-XXX — Control Test

EV-XXX — Evidence

F-XXX — Finding

EX-XXX — Exception

ACT-XXX — Treatment Action

This enables traceability from enterprise risk through technical
implementation and assurance.

Example:

R-002 Excessive Privilege
        ↓
AST-007 WIN-REC01
        ↓
CTRL-IAM-003 Least Privilege
        ↓
TEST-IAM-004 Reception Privilege Test
        ↓
EV-031 Test Output
        ↓
F-004 Excessive Local Privilege
        ↓
ACT-009 Remove Unnecessary Membership
        ↓
EV-032 Retest
        ↓
Residual Risk Reassessment
